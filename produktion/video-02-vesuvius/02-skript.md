## ACT 1 — THE LAST ORDINARY MORNING (0:00–~3:55)

[S01] **These two cities no longer exist. For the next seventeen minutes — they do.**

[COLD OPEN] The Bay of Naples, in the year seventy-nine. In the small hours of
the morning, in the town of Herculaneum, a household slave named Celer presses
a bronze stamp into the family's loaf of bread: CELER — SLAVE OF QUINTUS
GRANIUS VERUS. Mark the loaf. Send it to the oven. Start the day. He cannot
know that this bread will never be eaten — that in twenty-four hours, this
entire coastline will no longer exist. *(Beat, 1,5 s.)* Nearly nineteen
centuries later, archaeologists cut through twenty meters of solid rock — and
found his loaf. Blackened. Whole. The name
still legible on the crust. It lies in a museum in Naples today. This is the
story of the day it was baked.

[S02-S04] Two cities share this shoreline. Pompeii: loud, commercial, perhaps
twelve thousand people. Herculaneum: smaller, quieter, richer — a resort by
the sea. And above them both, green to the summit and covered in vineyards:
Vesuvius. Almost no one alive knows it is a volcano. One Greek geographer,
decades in his grave, read the scorched summit rocks correctly — a scholar's
footnote that never reached the people living below. No one in living memory
has seen the mountain burn; its last great eruption came centuries before
Rome existed. Spartacus once camped his rebel army in its quiet, overgrown
crater. To this bay, Vesuvius is simply where the good wine grows.

[TEASE] By this hour tomorrow, both cities will be gone — and at least two
thousand people will be dead. Buried so completely that the world forgets
these towns ever existed. For nearly seventeen hundred years.

[TRIKOLON] So walk their streets. Smell the bread. Live through the final
twenty-four hours of Pompeii and Herculaneum.

[S10 — TIME-SLIP] This is the forum of Pompeii today — maybe you've stood here
yourself. Now watch what AI lets us do. The ropes and ticket lines vanish. The
broken columns climb back to full height. The roofs close. The color floods
back — Roman cities were painted, loudly, everywhere. And the people return.
This is not a ruin anymore. This is a living town, on its very last morning.

[S11 — 999] One thing before we go in. Nine hundred ninety-nine details in

[S11b — CTA] While your eyes are scanning, do one thing for me. Millions have

[S12-S14 — WITNESSES] You already know our first witness: the man who stamped
the bread. Celer is real — his name is baked into the loaf itself, pressed
into the dough beside his owner's: Quintus Granius Verus. But a household
slave leaves no letters. So we have a second witness: across the bay,
thirty kilometers away, a seventeen-year-old who reads too much and questions
everything. His uncle commands the Roman fleet. When the mountain tears open,
the boy will watch every hour of it — and write it down. His letters survive;
every quote you hear today is his, word for word. Whether Celer walked out of

## ACT 2 — THE MOUNTAIN WAKES (~3:55–~9:10)

[S15-S18] Morning, Pompeii — glorious in its ordinariness. Fresh paint by the
gate: VOTE FOR HELVIUS SABINUS FOR AEDILE — there's an election to win. At
Asellina's corner bar, warm wine for the first customers. And at the bakery of
Modestus, eighty-one loaves slide into the oven. Remember those loaves.
Nobody will ever take them out.

[S19-S20] Everywhere you look: scaffolding. Seventeen years ago, an earthquake
cracked half of Pompeii, and the town is still rebuilding — fresh plaster,
stacked tiles, workmen on ladders. A city under construction, repairing
itself for a future it will never have.

*(Shot 31: Ambience-Fenster B, ~15 s — Marktstände. HIER liegt das falsche
Detail. Kein VO-Hinweis.)*

[S22-S23] For days, the ground has been restless — cups rattling, dogs barking
at nothing. Nobody panics. Our young witness later explained why in one dry
line: tremors were nothing unusual, because they happen in Campania all the
time. The bay has been whispering its warning for days. Nobody here speaks
the language.

[S24 — DATE DEBATE] One honest pause: what day is it? Every textbook says
August 24th — a date copied and re-copied by medieval scribes. But the ash
argues back: autumn fruit in the shops, braziers set out, heavier fabrics —
and in 2018, on a half-plastered wall, excavators found a workman's charcoal
note, dated to mid-October. August by the books. Autumn by the evidence. We
honestly don't know. What we do know, almost to the minute — is the hour.

[S25-S27 — ERUPTION] Around one o'clock in the afternoon, the mountain tears
itself open. The blast punches a column of gas and shattered rock into the
sky — and it keeps climbing. By evening it stands more than thirty kilometers
tall: three times the altitude a passenger jet flies. Across the bay, a seventeen-year-old stares at a shape nobody has a
word for yet — and reaches for the only comparison his world offers:

[LETTER 1 — Karte: FROM THE LETTERS OF PLINY THE YOUNGER · TO TACITUS, 6.16]
*"Its general appearance can best be expressed as being like an umbrella pine,
for it rose to a great height on a sort of trunk and then split off into
branches."*

[S28-S30 — PUMICE] Now forget every movie you've seen: there are no rivers of
glowing lava today. That is not how Vesuvius kills. The column overhead grinds
rock into froth — pumice, stone so full of gas it floats on water — and the
wind carries it southeast, directly over Pompeii. Stones begin to fall.
Fifteen centimeters an hour. Hour after hour. Not a hailstorm that passes —
a hailstorm with no end.

[S31-S32 — HERCULANEUM FLEES] And Herculaneum? It sits west of the mountain —
upwind. Almost nothing falls there; the town gets only the view: half the sky
replaced by a rising black wall. So most people make the smart choice, and
make it early: grab what fits in two hands, go down to the sea — to the stone
boat sheds along the beach, and wait for rescue by water. Keep those boat
sheds in mind. We'll be back at midnight.

[S33-S36 — THE ADMIRAL] In Misenum, the fleet's commander is the boy's uncle:
Pliny the Elder — the most famous scholar in Rome; his encyclopedia of the
natural world runs to thirty-seven volumes. His first instinct is a
scientist's: ready a fast ship, get closer. Then a message arrives — from
Rectina, a friend trapped in her villa at the mountain's foot, where escape
is only possible by sea. The research trip becomes a rescue mission. He
launches the fleet's warships and steers them straight into the falling
stone. His helmsman begs him to turn back. His answer survives, word for
word — in the Latin:

[LETTER 2 — Karte, Hauptzeile LATEIN: *"'Fortes' inquit 'fortuna iuvat:
Pomponianum pete.'"* (Plin. 6.16.11) · Subline, als Eigenübersetzung
gekennzeichnet: "Fortune favours the brave — make for Pomponianus."
(translation ours)]

[S37-S39 — STABIAE] They never reach Rectina — the shore is already choked
with debris — so he runs south to Stabiae, to his friend Pomponianus. Then,
one of the strangest scenes in the ancient sources: the old commander asks
for a bath. Then dinner. Then he sleeps — genuinely; his snoring, the boy
reports, could be heard through the door. Calm, or performance? Either way,
it steadied a terrified household. Outside, the stones did not stop.

[S40-S42 — THE CHOICE] Back in Pompeii: evening. The pumice in the streets is
past the knee. Roofs have begun to break. And every family faces the same
arithmetic with no good answer: stay under a groaning roof — or walk out into
falling stone? Those who go tie pillows over their heads, lashed down with
cloth — the same trick the boy's letters record that night down the coast,
at Stabiae. Those who stay tell each
other what people always tell each other: storms pass. This one will pass
too. *(Stille, 2 s.)*

## ACT 3 — THE NIGHT (~9:10–~14:20)

[S44-S46 — BOAT HOUSES] Midnight, Herculaneum. The town above is empty —
doors standing open, dinners abandoned on tables. Down at the waterline,
hundreds of people are packed together — on the open beach, and under the
stone arches of the boat sheds. Mothers with
infants. A soldier. Someone clutching a box of medical instruments. A woman
wearing her rings. They are doing the most human thing there is: staying
close to the water, watching the mountain burn, waiting for the boats.

[S47-S48 — COLLAPSE] Twelve hours in, the eruption changes. The column —
thirty kilometers of suspended rock — has been held up all day by sheer
thrust. When that thrust falters, physics presents the bill: the column
collapses under its own weight, and the fire comes down. A glowing avalanche
of gas, ash and rock — hundreds of degrees hot, faster than anything on this
coastline can run — pours down the western slope. Volcanologists call it a
pyroclastic surge. Herculaneum is in its path.

[S49-S50 — HERCULANEUM ENDS] It crosses the town in seconds — through the
empty streets, over the sea wall, into the boat sheds. *(Harter Schnitt auf
Schwarz. 3 s absolute Stille.)* For the people waiting there, it is over
faster than a thought. The heat is so extreme that nobody suffers — there is
no time to. Then the mountain seals the town. Not under four meters of ash,
like Pompeii. Under twenty. Herculaneum isn't buried tonight. It is erased.

[S51-S52 — 1982] Now mark this — one of the strangest turns in archaeology.
For more than two centuries after Herculaneum's rediscovery, excavators found almost
no one. A town of thousands — a handful of bodies. So the books wrote a
merciful ending: the people escaped. Then, in 1982, archaeologists reached
the ancient beach line and opened the boat sheds. Three hundred skeletons.
They had been there all along — at the edge of the water, waiting for help
that was still at sea.

[S53 — CELER] Was Celer among them? *(Beat.)* The honest answer: nobody
knows. We know only what he left behind — a stamped loaf in a fine house by
the sea wall, waiting for an owner who never came back. Hold that question.
The ash kept better records of bread than of the men who stamped it — but it
kept one more clue about this man. It waits at the end.

[S54-S56 — STABIAE, DER TOD] Down the coast at Stabiae, the commander's calm
runs out of road. The courtyard outside his room fills with pumice so fast
that his hosts wake him — much longer, and the door would never have opened
again. The house sways with shocks. They go down to the beach — by now, his
nephew notes, it is day everywhere else in the world; here, a black deeper
than any night. And the sea is wild, hopeless, the air turning: flames on
the horizon, the smell of sulfur. The old man lies down on a spread sailcloth and asks twice for
cold water. When the others finally run, he tries to stand, leaning on two
slaves — and collapses. Two days later, they find him on that beach:
unburned, unmarked — looking, his nephew wrote, more like a man asleep than
a man dead.

[S57-S59 — POMPEII, DAS ENDE] In Pompeii there is no sunrise on the second
day — only a black sky turning vaguely grey. Around dawn, the surges that
erased Herculaneum finally reach the second city. The first falter at the
edge of town. The next one does not. For everyone still inside — the ones
who waited out the storm that never passed — the story ends in a single hot
breath. *(Stille, 2 s.)* By mid-morning, where twelve thousand people lived
yesterday, there is a smoking grey plain, the tops of a few walls showing
through.

[S60-S61 — DIE ABGÜSSE] The ash did one more thing — something almost no
other disaster has left behind. It hardened around the fallen where they
lay; over the centuries the bodies faded away, but the shapes remained:
person-shaped hollows in the rock. Eighteen centuries later, an
archaeologist named Giuseppe Fiorelli poured plaster into those hollows —
and the people of Pompeii came back. Not skeletons. Postures. A man
shielding his face. Thirteen people together in a garden. A dog, still
chained. Their last second, held in plaster.

[S62-S65 — MISENUM] And the boy? On that second morning, it becomes his
turn: the cloud crosses the bay toward Misenum. The ground shakes carts
across level roads. And the sea itself does something he cannot explain:

[LETTER 3 — 6.20] *"We also saw the sea sucked away and apparently forced
back by the earthquake: at any rate it receded from the shore."*

Then the cloud comes down over the town — and daylight is cancelled:

[LETTER 4 — 6.20] *"Darkness fell, not the dark of a moonless or cloudy
night, but as if the lamp had been put out in a closed room."*

In that blackness: screaming — parents calling for children, voices
recognizing each other by sound alone. His mother begs him to leave her
behind; she is older, slower — save yourself. He refuses. He takes her hand.
Around them, people reach the only conclusion the evidence allows:

[LETTER 5 — 6.20] *"Many besought the aid of the gods, but still more
imagined there were no gods left, and that the universe was plunged into
eternal darkness for evermore."*

[S66] He is seventeen, choking on ash under a sky with no sun in it. Then,
slowly, the darkness thins. A weak light returns — a sun the color of an
eclipse. The world is still there. It is just grey: ash lying on everything,
he wrote, like snowdrifts. He and his mother survive.

## ACT 4 — WHAT THE ASH KEPT (~14:20–~17:30)

[S67-S68 — DIE NARBE] The eruption didn't just kill two cities — it redrew
the map. The coastline moved: where the sea once touched Herculaneum's boat
sheds, there is dry land today. And the mountain came out of that night
unrecognizable — it had blown its own summit apart. The elegant green cone
the Romans knew: gone. The jagged double profile above Naples today was born
in these twenty-four hours and the eruptions that followed. Every photo of
this bay you have ever seen — has the scar in it.

[S69-S71 — DAS VERGESSEN] Rome sent money and commissioners for the
survivors. But the cities were beyond help — and, in time, beyond memory.
Farmers planted vines over Pompeii. A new town grew directly on top of
Herculaneum, never knowing. For nearly seventeen hundred years: nothing.
Until the early 1700s, when workmen sinking a well shaft struck carved
marble, twenty meters down. Seats. Rows of seats. They had drilled into a
Roman theater. The world had found its time capsule.

[S72-S74 — DAS BROT] What came out of that rock is unlike anything else in
archaeology: whole streets, painted walls, furniture, an entire library of
carbonized scrolls. But let one object stand for all of it. In the 1930s, in
Herculaneum, in a fine seafront house called the House of the Deer,
excavators found a loaf of bread. Round. Scored into eight wedges. Burned to
charcoal — and stamped, still perfectly legible: CELER, SLAVE OF QUINTUS
GRANIUS VERUS. The loaf from the morning. It sits today in the National
Archaeological Museum in Naples — an object so ordinary that no one would
ever have kept it. Preserved by the one disaster terrible enough to do it.

[S75 — CELER-AUFLÖSUNG] So: did Celer survive? Here is every scrap history
holds, honestly weighed. His owner's family appears in Herculaneum's legal
tablets — and on a marble list of the town's citizens, some scholars read
the name of a freedman of that same household. A man who may once have
answered to Celer. If they are right, the slave who stamped this loaf lived.
Was freed. Grew old as a free man. *If* they are right — it is a name on a
stone, nothing more. But in a story where we know exactly how two thousand
people died, there is something fitting about the one man we followed:
history leaves his door open.

[S76 — PLINY-PAYOFF] The boy grew up — a lawyer, a senator, a governor. But
history remembers him for two letters, written to the historian Tacitus: the
ones you heard today. And when volcanologists watch an eruption column rise
on a trunk and split into branches — at St. Helens, at Pinatubo — they still
call it by its scientific name: a Plinian eruption. Named after a
seventeen-year-old who stood in the ash, watched — and wrote it down.
