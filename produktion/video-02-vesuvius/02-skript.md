## ACT 1 — THE LAST ORDINARY MORNING (0:00–~3:45)

[S01] **These two cities no longer exist. For the next seventeen minutes —
they do.**

[COLD OPEN] The Bay of Naples, in the year seventy-nine. Before dawn in
Herculaneum, a household slave named Celer presses a bronze stamp into the
family's loaf of bread: CELER — SLAVE OF QUINTUS GRANIUS VERUS. Mark the
loaf. Send it to the oven. Start the day. He cannot know that this loaf will
never be eaten — that in twenty-four hours, this kitchen, this house, this
entire coastline will no longer exist. *(Beat, 1,5 s.)* Nearly nineteen
centuries later, archaeologists cut through twenty meters of solid rock —
and found it. Blackened. Whole. His name still legible on the crust. This is
the story of the day it was baked.

[S02-S04] Two cities share this shoreline. Pompeii: loud, commercial,
perhaps twelve thousand people. Herculaneum: smaller, quieter, richer — a
resort by the sea. And above them both, green to the summit and covered in
vineyards: Vesuvius. Almost no one alive knows it is a volcano. No one in
living memory has seen the mountain burn. To this bay, it is simply where
the good wine grows. *(Shots 6-10 tragen das Strabo-Fußnoten-Bild —
Gelehrten-Ausnahme visuell, nicht im VO.)*

[TEASE] By this hour tomorrow, both cities will be gone — and at least two

[TRIKOLON] So walk their streets. Smell the bread. Watch the sky come down.
*(Beat.)* Two cities. One mountain. Twenty-four hours.

## ACT 2 — THE MOUNTAIN WAKES (~3:45–~9:30)

[S15-S18] Morning, Pompeii — and it looks like any morning anywhere. Someone
is running for office: fresh paint by the gate, VOTE FOR HELVIUS SABINUS FOR
AEDILE. Someone is opening a bar: warm wine for the first customers, the
menu scratched straight into the plaster — house wine, one small coin; the
good stuff, four. Breakfast prices, on the last day of the world. And
someone is baking: at the bakery of Modestus, eighty-one loaves slide into
the oven. Remember those loaves — nobody will ever take them out. None of
this feels like ancient history. It feels like a Tuesday.

[S19-S20] Everywhere you look: scaffolding. Seventeen years after a great
earthquake, Pompeii is still rebuilding — fresh plaster, stacked tiles, a
city repairing itself for a future it will never have.

*(Shot 31: Ambience-Fenster B, ~15 s — Marktstände. HIER liegt das falsche
Detail. Kein VO-Hinweis.)*

[S22-S23] For days, the ground has been restless — cups rattling, dogs
barking at nothing. Nobody panics. Our young witness later explained why, in
one dry line: earthquakes in Campania are nothing unusual. *(Beat.)* This
mountain has been quiet so long that a hundred and fifty years ago, Spartacus
camped his rebel army in its overgrown crater. The bay has been whispering
its warning for days. Nobody here speaks the language.

[S24 — DATE DEBATE] And — what day is it, exactly? Every textbook says
August 24th, a date copied by medieval scribes. But the ash argues back:
autumn fruit in the shops, braziers set out, heavier fabrics — and in 2018,
a workman's charcoal note on a half-plastered wall, dated mid-October.
August by the books. Autumn by the evidence. We honestly don't know. What we
do know, almost to the minute — is the hour.

[S25-S27 — ERUPTION] Around one in the afternoon, the mountain tears itself
open. The blast punches a column of gas and shattered rock into the sky —
and it keeps climbing. By evening it will stand more than thirty kilometers
tall: three times the altitude a passenger jet flies. Across the bay, the
boy reaches for the only comparison his world has:

[LETTER 1 — Karte: FROM THE LETTERS OF PLINY THE YOUNGER · TO TACITUS, 6.16]
*"Its general appearance can best be expressed as being like an umbrella
pine, for it rose to a great height on a sort of trunk and then split off
into branches."*

[S28-S30 — PUMICE] Forget the movies: there are no rivers of glowing lava
today. That is not how Vesuvius kills. The column overhead grinds rock into
froth — pumice, stone so full of gas it floats on water — and the wind
carries it southeast, over Pompeii. Stones begin to fall. On the roof tiles
it sounds like rain. Then like gravel. Then like fists. Fifteen centimeters
an hour — ankle-deep before the first hour is out. Not a hailstorm that
passes — a hailstorm with no end.

[S31-S32 — HERCULANEUM FLEES] And Herculaneum? It sits upwind — almost
nothing falls there. The town gets only the view: half the sky becoming a
black wall. Most choose early, and well: grab what fits in two hands, go
down to the beach, to the stone boat sheds. Keep that beach in mind. We'll
be back at midnight.

[S33-S36 — THE ADMIRAL] In Misenum, the fleet's commander is the boy's
uncle: Pliny the Elder — Rome's most famous scholar, thirty-seven volumes to
his name. His first instinct is a scientist's — get closer. Then a message:
Rectina, a friend trapped at the mountain's foot — escape only by sea. The
research trip becomes a rescue mission. He launches the warships — straight
into the falling stone. His helmsman begs him to turn back. His answer
survives, word for word — in the Latin:

[LETTER 2 — Karte, Hauptzeile LATEIN: *"'Fortes' inquit 'fortuna iuvat:
Pomponianum pete.'"* (Plin. 6.16.11) · Subline, gekennzeichnet: "Fortune
favours the brave — make for Pomponianus." (translation ours)]

[S37-S39 — STABIAE] They never reach Rectina — the shore is choked with
debris — so he runs south, to Stabiae, to his friend Pomponianus. Then, one
of the strangest scenes antiquity recorded: the old commander asks for a
bath. Then dinner. Then he sleeps — genuinely; his snoring, the boy reports,
was heard through the door. Calm, or performance? Either way, it steadied
the room. Outside, the stones did not stop.

[S40-S42 — THE CHOICE] Back in Pompeii: evening. The pumice in the streets
is past the knee. Roofs have begun to break. Every family now faces the same
arithmetic: stay under a groaning roof — or walk out into falling stone?
Those who go tie pillows over their heads — a trick the boy's letters record
that same night at Stabiae. Those who stay tell each other what people
always say: storms pass. This one will pass too. *(Stille, 2 s.)*

[S43 — AUFLÖSUNG VIDEO 1] Before night falls — we owe you an answer.
Constantinople. The planted mistake. *(3-s-Zoom auf V1-Shot #45.)* There, on

## ACT 3 — THE NIGHT (~9:30–~14:55)

[S44-S46 — BOAT HOUSES] Midnight, Herculaneum. The town above is empty —
doors standing open, dinners abandoned on tables. Down at the waterline,
hundreds of people are packed together — on the open beach, and under the
stone arches of the boat sheds. Mothers with infants. A soldier. A woman
wearing her rings. Doing the most human thing there is: staying close to the
water, watching the mountain burn, waiting for the boats.

[S47-S48 — COLLAPSE] Twelve hours in, the eruption changes. The column —
thirty kilometers of suspended rock — has been held up all day by nothing
but thrust. When that engine falters, physics presents the bill: the column
collapses under its own weight — and the fire comes down. A glowing
avalanche, hundreds of degrees hot, faster than anything alive, pours down
the western slope. Volcanologists call it a pyroclastic surge. Herculaneum
is in its path.

[S49-S50 — HERCULANEUM ENDS] It crosses the town in seconds — over the sea
wall, onto the beach. *(Harter Schnitt auf Schwarz. 3 s absolute Stille.)*
For the people waiting there, it is over faster than a thought. The heat is
so extreme that nobody suffers — there is no time to. Then the mountain
seals the town. Not under four meters of ash, like Pompeii. Under twenty —
six stories of rock. Herculaneum isn't buried tonight. It is erased.

[S51-S52 — 1982] Now, the strangest turn in this whole story. For more than
two centuries after Herculaneum's rediscovery, excavators found almost no
one. A town of thousands — a handful of bodies. So the books wrote a
merciful ending: the people escaped. Then, in 1982, archaeologists reached
the ancient beach line. Three hundred skeletons. They had been there all
along — waiting for help that was still at sea.

[S53 — CELER] Was Celer among them? *(Beat.)* The honest answer: nobody
knows. We know only what he left behind — a stamped loaf in a fine house by
the sea wall, waiting for an owner who never came back. The ash kept better
records of bread than of the men who stamped it. One more clue about him
waits at the end.

[S53b — RE-HOOK] Herculaneum is gone. But the night is not done choosing.
Down the coast, the admiral sleeps in a house filling up like an hourglass —
and across the bay, the boy writing it all down is next in the cloud's path.

[S54-S56 — STABIAE, DER TOD] At Stabiae, the courtyard outside the
commander's room fills with pumice so fast that his hosts wake him — any
longer, and the door would not have opened. They go down to the beach — by
now, his nephew notes, it is day everywhere else in the world; here, a black
deeper than any night. The sea is wild and hopeless. And now the air itself
turns on them: flame on the horizon, and then the smell reaches the beach —
sulfur. *(Pause, 1 s.)* The old man lies down on a sailcloth, asks twice for
cold water. When the others finally run, he tries to stand — and collapses.
Two days later, they find him: unburned, unmarked — looking, his nephew
wrote, more like a man asleep than a man dead.

[S57-S59 — POMPEII, DAS ENDE] In Pompeii, no sunrise comes — only black
turning grey. Around that same hour, the surges that erased Herculaneum
reach the second city. The first falter at the edge of town. The next one
does not. For everyone still inside, the story ends in a single hot breath.
*(Stille, 2 s.)* By mid-morning, where twelve thousand people lived
yesterday, there is only a smoking grey plain.

[S62-S65 — MISENUM] And the boy? On that second morning, it is his turn: the
cloud crosses the bay toward Misenum. The ground shakes carts across level
roads. And the sea itself does something he cannot explain — it pulls back,
draining away from the shore, stranding sea creatures on the dry sand. Then
the cloud comes down, and daylight is cancelled:

[LETTER 4 — 6.20] *"Darkness fell, not the dark of a moonless or cloudy
night, but as if the lamp had been put out in a closed room."*

In that blackness: screaming — parents calling for children, voices finding
each other by sound alone. His mother begs him to leave her behind; she is
older, slower — save yourself. He refuses. He takes her hand. Around them,
people reach the only conclusion the evidence allows:

[LETTER 5 — 6.20] *"Many besought the aid of the gods, but still more
imagined there were no gods left, and that the universe was plunged into
eternal darkness for evermore."*

[S66] He is seventeen, choking on ash under a sky with no sun. Then, slowly,
the darkness thins. A weak light returns — a sun the color of an eclipse.
Ash lies on everything, he wrote, like snowdrifts. He and his mother
survive.

## ACT 4 — WHAT THE ASH KEPT (~14:55–~19:30)

[S66b — BRÜCKE] One of our witnesses walks out of this story alive. The
other left us a loaf of bread — and one last clue, still waiting. But first:
look what the mountain did to the world itself.

[S67-S68 — DIE NARBE] The eruption redrew the map. Where the sea once
touched Herculaneum's boat sheds is dry land today. And the mountain came
out unrecognizable — it had blown its own summit apart. The jagged double
profile above Naples was born in these twenty-four hours and the eruptions
that followed. Every photo of this bay has the scar in it.

[S69-S71 — DAS VERGESSEN] Farmers planted vines over Pompeii. A new town
grew on top of Herculaneum, never knowing. For nearly seventeen hundred
years: nothing. Until the early 1700s, when workmen sinking a well struck
carved marble, twenty meters down. Seats. Rows of seats. A Roman theater.
The world had found its time capsule.

[S72-S74 — DAS BROT + ABGÜSSE] What came out of that rock is unlike anything
else on Earth. Whole streets. Painted walls. A library of carbonized
scrolls. In Pompeii, the people themselves: hollows in the hardened ash that
Giuseppe Fiorelli filled with plaster — a man shielding his face, thirteen
people in a garden, a dog still chained. And the eighty-one loaves at
Modestus' bakery? Excavators found them still in the oven — eighteen
centuries late. But let one object stand for it all: in the 1930s, in a
seafront house called the House of the Deer — a loaf of bread. Round. Scored
into eight wedges. Burned to charcoal — and stamped, still perfectly
legible: CELER, SLAVE OF QUINTUS GRANIUS VERUS. The loaf from the morning.
It lies today in a museum in Naples — an object so ordinary that no one
would ever have kept it. *(Beat.)* Nobody keeps a loaf of bread. It took the
end of a world to keep this one.

[S75 — CELER-AUFLÖSUNG] So: did Celer survive? Here is every scrap history
holds. His owner's family appears in Herculaneum's legal tablets — and on a
marble list of citizens, some scholars read a freedman of that same
household: a man who may once have answered to Celer.
If they are right, the slave who stamped this loaf lived. Was freed. Grew
old as a free man. *If* they are right — it is a name on a stone, nothing
more. But in a story where we know exactly how two thousand people died,
history leaves his door open.

[S76 — PLINY-PAYOFF] The boy grew up: lawyer, senator, governor. History
remembers two letters, written to Tacitus — the ones you heard today. And when volcanologists watch an eruption column rise on a
trunk and split into branches — St. Helens, Pinatubo — they still call it a
Plinian eruption. Named for a seventeen-year-old who stood in the ash,
watched — and wrote it down.
